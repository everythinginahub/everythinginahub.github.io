A PRACTICAL GCSE REVISION GUIDE

How do I make a revision timetable for GCSEs?

Start with your real exam dates and the time you actually have. A useful timetable gives each subject a manageable next step, makes room for breaks and can change when your week does—it does not need to fill every hour.

1. Start with exam dates and your real week

Before you add revision sessions, gather your school’s exam timetable, your subject list and the specification or topic checklist for each exam board. Note important coursework or mock deadlines too. A GCSE timetable is only useful if it reflects the subjects and dates you actually have, rather than a generic plan made for someone else.

Next, sketch the week you already live: lessons, travel, clubs, caring responsibilities, meals, sleep and time to relax. Mark the spaces where you could realistically study. Leave some unplanned time for rest and unexpected changes; a plan that depends on being free every evening is likely to be hard to keep.

Quick answer: list exam dates, split each subject into topics, choose a few realistic study slots, decide what you will do in each one, then review and adjust the plan weekly.

2. Break each subject into topics and choose priorities

Use the course specification, teacher’s checklist, class notes or revision guide to make a topic list for each subject. Keep the topics small enough to revise in one sitting: “practise simultaneous equations” is easier to plan than “revise all of maths.” If a subject has separate papers, note which topics belong to each one.

For each topic, make a quick note of your confidence and the next exam date. You might use labels such as “ready to practise,” “needs another look” and “not started.” These are planning prompts, not grades. Give attention to topics that need more practice and exams coming up sooner, while still returning to topics you already know so they stay fresh.

Check what is on your course

Exam-board specifications can differ. Use the one that matches your class and ask your teacher if you are unsure what a topic label means.

Make a task specific

Replace “do science” with a clear action, such as drawing a process from memory or answering a short set of questions on one topic.

Prioritise without panic

Use exam timing and your own confidence to choose what comes next. A low-confidence label is a cue to practise, not a prediction of your result.

3. Choose manageable sessions and spread them out

Put study slots into the available spaces you marked, starting with a small number you can protect. You do not have to revise every subject every day. A session can be short; choose a length that fits the task and your concentration, and take a proper break between blocks. If your schedule is busy, a few focused sessions across the week may be more realistic than a long catch-up day.

Where time allows, revisit a topic on more than one day instead of trying to learn everything in one sitting. Space the sessions around your other subjects and leave time to check what you remembered. Put the most demanding work at a time when you are usually able to focus, and keep some easier recall or review tasks for shorter gaps.

Be cautious about copying a timetable that promises a fixed number of daily hours. Your age, energy, school day and responsibilities matter. If you are not sure how to balance revision with sleep, wellbeing or schoolwork, talk it through with a teacher or a trusted adult.

4. Give every slot a clear revision activity

Write down what you will do, not just the subject name. A specific task makes it easier to begin and to tell whether the session helped. Try to retrieve information, solve problems or explain an idea before checking your notes; then use your materials to correct gaps.

For maths and sciences

Work through questions without looking at the worked answer first. Mark your attempt, write down the step you missed and try a similar question later.

For English and humanities

Recall key quotations, dates or evidence, then practise a short essay plan or explain how an example supports your point.

For languages

Practise vocabulary both ways, make a short sentence from memory or review a grammar point and use it in your own example.

Use past-paper questions and mark schemes that match your exam board and the stage of your course, following your teacher’s guidance. A quiz or flashcard set can help with a quick check, but it may not cover the full specification.

A simple revision timetable GCSE example

If you searched for “revision timetable GCSE”, use this outline as a starting point rather than a schedule to copy exactly. Move the days, subjects and session lengths around your own lessons, exam dates and commitments.

  1. Monday: one biology topic—draw or explain the main idea from memory, then check your notes.
  2. Tuesday: a short maths question set—mark it and note one skill to revisit.
  3. Wednesday: keep the evening free, or use a short catch-up slot if a session moved.
  4. Thursday: English literature—recall a few useful quotations and plan one paragraph.
  5. Saturday: chemistry practice questions, followed by a quick look at next week’s priorities. Keep time for breaks and the rest of your weekend.
Make the example yours. Swap in your subjects and topics, check the exam dates, and reduce the number of sessions if the plan feels too full. A timetable is a guide you can revise, not a test of willpower.

5. Review the plan each week and adjust it

At the end of the week, check what you completed and what still needs attention. Move unfinished work to a realistic open slot instead of doubling every session the next day. If a topic is going well, keep it in rotation occasionally; if you are stuck, break it into a smaller question and ask a teacher for help.

Make a fresh plan for the next seven days based on new homework, lessons and exam information. Protect rest as part of the plan. If you fall behind, the useful next step is to prioritise and restart—not to treat one missed session as proof that the whole timetable has failed.

GCSE revision timetable questions

How many subjects should I revise in one day?

There is no single right number. Pick a manageable amount that fits your day and gives you time to focus. A clear task for one or two subjects can be more useful than rushing through a long list.

When should I start making a GCSE revision timetable?

Start when you have enough information about your subjects, topics and upcoming assessments to choose a next step. Keep the plan flexible as your school shares dates and course guidance.

What should I do if I miss a planned session?

Move it if it is still a priority, or choose a smaller task for the next available slot. Avoid trying to recover every missed minute by cutting into sleep or essential rest.

Can Everything Hub make my revision timetable automatically?

No. The planner helps you organise tasks and regular lessons, while you decide how to schedule revision. The guide and tools support your plan but do not replace your course specification or teacher advice.
